# Golf Cart Usage and Accessibility Guidelines

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document is designed to help tournament organizers make informed decisions about **golf cart usage and accessibility**. It is not simply a set of cart rules for players. Its purpose is to help an organizer think through how carts should be allocated, managed, restricted, and communicated in a way that supports safety, pace of play, player comfort, course protection, and equitable access.

Golf carts are one of the most visible operational elements of a tournament. Poor cart planning creates immediate problems: slow starts, confusion at check-in, disputes about assignments, course damage, accessibility failures, and frustration among players or guests. Strong cart planning, by contrast, quietly improves the entire event.

A tournament organizer should therefore treat cart policy as part of event design, not as a small logistical detail.

---

## 2. Core Decision Principle

A good cart policy balances five things:

* **player mobility and comfort**
* **accessibility and accommodation**
* **course safety and turf protection**
* **pace of play and operational flow**
* **fairness and consistency**

The right cart policy depends on the type of event, the player population, the course layout, the weather, and the operational standards of the facility. There is no single cart policy that is correct for every tournament. The goal is to adopt the policy that best fits the event’s actual needs.

---

## 3. Start With the Event Type

Cart decisions should be driven first by the nature of the tournament.

### A. Charity and Corporate Events

These events usually prioritize comfort, social interaction, sponsor experience, and accessibility for a broad range of players.

**Typical cart approach:**

* carts included for all players
* two riders per cart unless otherwise required
* generous accommodation planning
* emphasis on smooth staging and clear labeling

### B. Member Tournaments and Club Events

These events may balance comfort and competitive structure. Some fields will expect carts, while others may include players who prefer to walk.

**Typical cart approach:**

* carts available to most players
* walking permitted unless restricted by the course
* accommodations handled by request and in coordination with the club

### C. Competitive Stroke Play or Championship Events

These events may need a more deliberate policy because cart access can affect competitive norms, pace, and fairness expectations.

**Typical cart approach:**

* player cart access determined by event rules and course policy
* carts often reserved for staff, referees, or players with approved accommodations when the event is primarily walking-based
* strong need for consistency in administration

### Policy Recommendation

Choose the cart model based on the event’s purpose. Do not assume that the right cart policy for a corporate outing is also right for a championship event.

---

## 4. Decide Whether Carts Are Included, Optional, or Restricted

The organizer should make an early decision about the baseline cart policy.

### Option 1: Carts Included for All Players

**Best for:** charity events, corporate outings, mixed-ability social tournaments
**Advantages:** simple, inclusive, easy to market, strong player comfort
**Challenges:** higher cost, more cart fleet demand, more staging work

### Option 2: Carts Optional

**Best for:** club events, member tournaments, smaller mixed fields
**Advantages:** offers flexibility, may control cost, accommodates both walkers and riders
**Challenges:** requires a fair reservation process, can complicate pairings and logistics

### Option 3: Carts Restricted or Limited

**Best for:** championships, walking-focused competitions, courses with fleet or terrain limits
**Advantages:** preserves competitive norms, protects course and fleet availability
**Challenges:** requires clear communication, stronger accommodation planning, greater sensitivity around access decisions

### Policy Recommendation

Set the default cart policy early and communicate it clearly in registration materials. Players should know whether carts are included, available at extra cost, limited, or restricted before they commit to the event.

---

## 5. Understand the Course and Facility Constraints

The cart policy should reflect the actual physical realities of the host facility.

### Questions to Ask the Course

* How many carts are available for tournament use?
* Are there enough carts for the full field plus staff needs?
* Are there restrictions on cart use due to weather or turf conditions?
* Are there cart path only policies under certain conditions?
* Are there holes or areas where carts are prohibited?
* Is the course especially difficult to walk due to distance or terrain?
* Are there charging, staging, or fleet turnover limitations?

### Why This Matters

A course with a limited cart fleet, steep terrain, long green-to-tee transfers, or frequent wet conditions may require a very different policy than a flatter facility with abundant inventory.

### Policy Recommendation

Cart policy should be built in coordination with course operations. The organizer should not promise more cart access than the facility can reliably support.

---

## 6. Build Accessibility Into the Policy From the Start

Accessibility should not be treated as an exception that is handled informally at the last minute. It should be part of the event plan from the beginning.

### Accessibility Considerations May Include

* players with mobility limitations
* older participants who need riding support
* temporary injuries
* medical conditions affecting endurance or walking tolerance
* staff, volunteers, or guests who require mobility assistance
* access to staging, registration, dining, and awards areas in addition to the golf course itself

### Important Organizer Mindset

The question is not simply whether a person can technically get around the course. The question is whether the event design provides a reasonable and dignified way for eligible participants to take part fully and safely.

### Policy Recommendation

Include an accessibility request field in registration and create a documented process for handling those requests consistently and respectfully.

---

## 7. Create a Formal Accommodation Process

A tournament organizer should not rely on ad hoc cart decisions made at the bag drop or first tee.

### A Good Accommodation Process Should Include

* a clear method for participants to request accommodation in advance
* a designated contact person or decision-maker
* a timeline for when requests should be submitted
* coordination with course staff before the event
* privacy and discretion in handling sensitive information
* written confirmation of the accommodation arrangement when appropriate

### Practical Registration Language

Registration materials should include a clear invitation for participants to identify mobility or accessibility needs that may require cart access or other operational support.

### Policy Recommendation

Accessibility arrangements should be planned early enough that they do not depend on leftover cart inventory on event day.

---

## 8. Distinguish Between Player Carts and Operational Carts

Not every cart in the fleet serves the same purpose. Organizers should think in categories.

### Common Cart Categories

* player carts
* VIP or sponsor carts
* rules official carts
* marshal carts
* medical or emergency response carts
* volunteer support carts
* photography or media carts if applicable
* beverage or hospitality service carts

### Why This Matters

If every cart is assumed to be available for players, the event may find itself short on the operational support that keeps the tournament functioning smoothly.

### Policy Recommendation

Reserve operational carts intentionally before finalizing player cart allocations.

---

## 9. Decide on Rider Capacity and Assignment Logic

Once the general cart policy is set, the organizer should determine how carts will actually be assigned.

### Standard Practice

Most tournaments assign **two riders per cart**.

### Questions to Resolve

* Will teammates share carts or will pairings determine cart assignments?
* Will walking players be paired with riders operationally or separated where possible?
* Will single-rider carts ever be permitted, and under what conditions?
* How will accommodation-based single-rider use affect fleet capacity?

### Common Assignment Models

#### Pairing-Based Assignment

Cart assignments follow playing groups.

**Best for:** straightforward tournament operations
**Pros:** simple and easy to stage
**Cons:** may not align with player preferences or accessibility needs

#### Team-Based Assignment

Players on the same team share cart arrangements.

**Best for:** scramble and social team events
**Pros:** supports social experience and team interaction
**Cons:** may complicate flow if team structure differs from starting order or cart staging

### Policy Recommendation

Choose one assignment logic and apply it consistently. Avoid making large numbers of custom exceptions unless the event is small enough to manage them carefully.

---

## 10. Plan for Single-Rider Cart Situations

Some events will need a limited number of single-rider carts, particularly for accessibility reasons.

### When Single-Rider Carts May Be Appropriate

* approved mobility accommodations
* operational staff roles that require independent movement
* on-course medical support
* rules officials in certain competitive events

### Why This Needs Planning

Single-rider carts reduce total carrying capacity and can create shortages quickly if not managed deliberately.

### Policy Recommendation

Single-rider carts should be approved intentionally, not distributed casually. Build them into the fleet plan before the field is finalized.

---

## 11. Account for Walking Players if Allowed

If the event allows walking, the organizer must decide how walking and riding players will coexist.

### Questions to Consider

* Is walking truly practical on this course for this field?
* Will walking players slow the event or create pairing strain?
* Will walkers have access to the same staging and support as riders?
* Does the course or tournament format make mixed walking/riding groups awkward?

### When Mixed Walking and Riding Works Best

* smaller or moderately sized fields
* competitive events where walking is culturally normal
* courses with manageable terrain and distances

### When It Can Be Problematic

* social foursome-heavy events
* large fields with aggressive tee sheets
* very spread-out or hilly courses
* events where carts are central to the player experience

### Policy Recommendation

If walking is permitted, communicate that clearly and think through how it affects pairings, pace, and staging. Do not treat walking as a neutral variable if the rest of the event is built around cart use.

---

## 12. Use Cart Policy to Support Pace of Play

Carts can either help pace or hurt it depending on how they are used.

### Ways Carts Improve Pace

* reduce transit time between shots and holes
* help older or less mobile players keep up
* support staff monitoring and intervention
* make long transfers more manageable

### Ways Carts Can Hurt Pace

* poor rider coordination leads to inefficiency
* shared carts split players far from their balls
* excessive traffic near greens and tees creates congestion
* unclear rules about where to drive cause delays
* cart path only conditions can slow play significantly

### Policy Recommendation

Do not assume that more carts automatically mean faster play. Pace improves only when the cart policy matches the course layout, player type, and operating rules.

---

## 13. Prepare for Cart Path Only and Wet Weather Conditions

Weather can change the functional cart policy even if the original plan was sound.

### Operational Risks in Wet Conditions

* turf damage
* unsafe slopes or braking conditions
* backed-up pace under cart path only rules
* player frustration if expectations were not set in advance

### Good Advance Planning Includes

* asking the course how wet-weather cart decisions are made
* identifying how a cart path only policy will be communicated
* considering how accessibility accommodations will still be honored if restrictions apply
* building time and flexibility into the day if severe conditions are possible

### Policy Recommendation

The tournament’s cart policy should include contingency language for cart restrictions driven by weather or course protection needs.

---

## 14. Protect Safety Around Cart Use

Cart policy is also a safety policy.

### Safety Topics the Organizer Should Think Through

* who is allowed to drive carts
* minimum driver age if relevant
* alcohol-related cart risk at social events
* safe speed and courtesy expectations
* rules near tees, greens, bridges, and steep slopes
* passenger limits
* procedures for accidents or breakdowns

### Why This Matters

Many tournaments are relaxed socially, but relaxed tone should not mean loose safety standards.

### Policy Recommendation

Adopt and communicate a simple set of cart safety expectations in pre-event materials and on-site announcements, especially for social or sponsor-heavy tournaments.

---

## 15. Consider Fairness in Competitive Events

In some tournaments, especially competitive walking-based events, cart access may raise fairness questions. The organizer should think about those questions early.

### Competitive Fairness Issues May Include

* whether the event is intended to be a walking competition
* whether some players are receiving cart access while others are not
* how approved accommodations are handled consistently
* whether the event’s governing framework or competitive expectations limit cart use

### Important Planning Principle

Fairness does not mean treating every participant identically in every operational detail. It means applying the event policy consistently while handling legitimate accessibility needs in a structured and respectful way.

### Policy Recommendation

If the event is competitively sensitive, document the cart policy and accommodation process clearly enough that decisions can be explained and defended if questions arise.

---

## 16. Make Cart Staging Part of the Event Design

Cart usage is not just about whether carts exist. It is also about how they are staged, labeled, and deployed.

### Good Cart Staging Includes

* cart signs with player names or team names
* organized staging by tee, wave, or starting hole
* separate handling for VIP or operational carts
* accessible placement for participants who need easier access
* clear routing between registration, bag drop, practice areas, and the first tee

### Operational Value

Well-staged carts reduce check-in congestion, prevent assignment errors, and make the event feel organized from the moment players arrive.

### Policy Recommendation

Treat cart staging as part of tournament operations, not as a last-minute parking exercise.

---

## 17. Coordinate Cart Policy With Tee Time or Shotgun Start Structure

Cart planning must match the start model.

### Tee Time Events

Need:

* clear cart labeling by time and group
* steady release to the tee
* procedures for late arrivals and unused carts

### Shotgun Start Events

Need:

* organized distribution across starting holes
* attention to traffic patterns before the horn
* time for players to travel from staging to assigned holes
* clear return expectations after the round

### Policy Recommendation

A cart plan that works for tee times may fail under a shotgun start if distribution and travel time are not rethought. Always match cart operations to the event’s starting format.

---

## 18. Build Cost Implications Into the Decision

Cart policy has budget consequences and should be evaluated accordingly.

### Potential Cost Factors

* cart rental fees
* premium cart requirements for VIPs or sponsors
* extra single-rider carts
* staff and operational carts
* accessibility support needs
* damage, cleanup, or fleet management charges if applicable

### Wrong Approach

“Including carts sounds easier, so let’s just do it.”

### Better Approach

“Here is how including, limiting, or charging separately for carts affects player experience, accessibility, pace, and cost.”

### Policy Recommendation

Cart policy should be a conscious tradeoff decision, not an unexamined default.

---

## 19. Communicate Cart Expectations Clearly to Players

Many event-day frustrations happen because the organizer assumed players knew how the cart plan worked.

### Player Communication Should Clarify

* whether carts are included, optional, or restricted
* whether players must request carts in advance
* whether walking is allowed
* how accommodations should be requested
* whether cart path only restrictions may occur
* where carts will be staged
* when players should report for loading or departure

### Policy Recommendation

Do not let cart expectations live only in a course contract or internal spreadsheet. Publish them to participants clearly and early.

---

## 20. Use a Decision Framework Before Finalizing the Policy

A tournament organizer should work through the following questions in order:

1. What kind of event is this: social, mixed, or highly competitive?
2. What does the course physically support in terms of fleet size, terrain, and conditions?
3. Are carts included, optional, or restricted by default?
4. What accessibility and accommodation needs should be anticipated?
5. How many carts must be reserved for operations before player allocation begins?
6. Will walking be allowed, and if so, how will mixed groups be managed?
7. How will single-rider carts be handled?
8. How will cart assignments be staged and communicated?
9. What weather-related cart restrictions might alter the plan?
10. How does the cart policy affect cost, pace, fairness, and player experience?

This is the correct order. Do not start with whatever cart inventory happens to remain and build the policy backward from there.

---

## 21. Recommended Default Policies by Event Type

### Charity or Corporate Tournament

* include carts for all players
* assign two riders per cart by default
* reserve dedicated accessibility and operational carts early
* use visible cart labeling and organized staging
* communicate weather-related restrictions in advance

### Member Tournament

* decide whether carts are included or optional based on club norms and field type
* allow accommodation requests through registration
* balance walkers and riders thoughtfully
* reserve operational carts before player assignment

### Competitive Stroke Play Event

* align cart access with event rules and competitive expectations
* create a documented accommodation process
* use carts deliberately for officials, medical support, and approved needs
* communicate the policy precisely to avoid fairness disputes

### Premium Invitational

* prioritize comfort and smooth experience
* use generous staging and labeling
* consider single-rider exceptions only if fleet capacity supports them
* make accessibility support highly visible and proactive

---

## 22. Common Mistakes to Avoid

* assuming the course has enough carts for every player and staff role
* treating accessibility requests informally or too late
* using leftover inventory rather than planned allocation
* failing to reserve operational carts
* not planning for wet-weather restrictions
* allowing too many unplanned single-rider carts
* mixing walkers and riders without thinking through pace and pairing effects
* failing to label and stage carts clearly
* communicating cart policy too late for players to plan around it

---

## 23. Recommended Default Guideline

If an organizer wants a strong general starting point, the following is a practical default:

* Set the baseline cart policy early: **included, optional, or restricted**.
* Build an **advance accommodation request process** into registration.
* Reserve carts for **operations and accessibility** before assigning the remaining fleet to players.
* Default to **two riders per cart**, with single-rider carts used only when operationally necessary or as part of an approved accommodation.
* Coordinate the policy with the course’s **fleet limits, terrain, and weather procedures**.
* Communicate clearly whether **walking is allowed**, how cart assignments work, and how restrictions may change in poor conditions.
* Treat cart staging, labeling, and distribution as a core part of tournament operations.

---

## 24. Final Advice

A good golf cart policy is not really about carts. It is about designing an event that works for real people on a real course under real conditions.

The organizer’s goal should be to create a cart plan that is safe, accessible, practical, and aligned with the event’s purpose. When done well, players barely notice the policy because everything feels natural: the right people have the right mobility support, the course does not feel congested, the start runs smoothly, and the event feels organized.

That is the standard to aim for. Not maximum cart usage, and not minimum administrative effort, but a thoughtful operating policy that supports the tournament as a whole.
