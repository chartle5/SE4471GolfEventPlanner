# Pace of Play Guidelines

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This document is designed to help tournament organizers make informed decisions about **pace of play**. It is not simply a reminder for players to “play faster.” Its purpose is to help an organizer design an event that supports steady pace from the first group to the last, establish realistic expectations, identify the operational causes of delay, and decide how to respond when the field falls behind.

Pace of play is one of the strongest drivers of tournament quality. When pace is managed well, the event feels organized, fair, and enjoyable. When pace breaks down, nearly every part of the tournament suffers: player experience declines, scoring becomes uneven, weather and daylight risks increase, staff fatigue rises, meals and awards run late, and frustration spreads across the field.

A tournament organizer should therefore treat pace of play as a design issue, not merely a player behavior issue.

---

## 2. Core Decision Principle

A strong pace-of-play policy balances five things:

* **player experience**
* **competitive fairness**
* **course capacity and flow**
* **operational control**
* **reasonable expectations for the field type**

The goal is not to make players feel rushed. The goal is to keep the tournament moving at a pace that is steady, fair, and appropriate for the format and the course.

A good pace policy recognizes that slow play usually does not come from one cause. It usually comes from the interaction of scheduling, format, field ability, course setup, scoring demands, staffing, and player habits.

---

## 3. Start With the Type of Event

Pace expectations should be based on the kind of tournament being run.

### A. Charity and Corporate Events

These events often include mixed-skill players, more social interaction, sponsor activity, and occasional on-course contests.

**Typical pace reality:** slower than a competitive member event
**Primary organizer goal:** preserve enjoyment while preventing the round from dragging excessively

### B. Member Tournaments and Club Events

These events often sit between social and competitive play. Pace will depend heavily on the field makeup and format.

**Typical pace reality:** moderate, but highly sensitive to format and tee-time structure
**Primary organizer goal:** keep the event moving without making the competition feel rushed

### C. Competitive Stroke Play or Championship Events

These events often require more time per shot, more score verification, more rules awareness, and more deliberate decision-making.

**Typical pace reality:** not necessarily fast, but it should be disciplined and predictable
**Primary organizer goal:** protect competitive integrity while preventing avoidable delay

### Policy Recommendation

Set pace standards that fit the actual event. Do not apply a casual outing mindset to a championship field, and do not apply championship expectations to a beginner-heavy fundraiser without adjustment.

---

## 4. Build Pace Into the Event Design, Not Just the Player Memo

A tournament cannot be designed in a way that invites delay and then expect a pace reminder to solve it.

### Design Choices That Strongly Affect Pace

* starting method
* tee time interval
* group size
* tournament format
* field skill level
* course setup
* number of contests or activations
* cart policy
* scoring procedure
* weather and turf restrictions

### Important Organizer Standard

If the event structure makes slow play likely, the pace policy should address the structure first, not blame players later.

### Policy Recommendation

Before publishing pace expectations, confirm that the event has been designed in a way that gives the field a realistic chance to meet them.

---

## 5. Choose the Starting Structure With Pace in Mind

Pace is heavily shaped by how the field enters the course.

### Tee Time Starts

**Advantages for pace:**

* natural spacing between groups
* easier to absorb field differences gradually
* stronger control over the first tee

**Risks for pace:**

* intervals that are too aggressive create congestion immediately
* large foursome-heavy fields can back up quickly

### Double Tee Starts

**Advantages for pace:**

* shorter overall starting window
* improved field capacity without a full shotgun

**Risks for pace:**

* different pace patterns on each side of the course
* turn conflicts if not planned carefully

### Shotgun Starts

**Advantages for pace:**

* synchronized start and finish
* strong event flow for meals and awards

**Risks for pace:**

* the entire field is compressed onto the course at once
* the pace of the slowest sections can affect everyone
* on-course contests or long transitions can create wider disruption

### Policy Recommendation

Choose the starting structure that matches the event and that the course can support at a steady pace. Do not choose a start model only for convenience if it will harm flow on the course.

---

## 6. Set Tee Time Intervals Conservatively Enough to Protect the Field

Tee time interval is one of the most important pace decisions in the entire tournament.

### Shorter Intervals

**Benefits:** greater capacity, shorter start window
**Risks:** early backups, less recovery room, more pressure on every group

### Longer Intervals

**Benefits:** better spacing, lower congestion risk, more comfortable start flow
**Risks:** longer day, reduced capacity

### Practical Guidance

* strong competitive field: shorter but still disciplined intervals may work
* mixed-ability member field: moderate intervals are safer
* social foursome-heavy field: conservative intervals are usually wiser

### Policy Recommendation

Choose intervals based on the field’s likely pace, not the organizer’s preferred player count. A slightly smaller field with steady play is usually better than a full field that collapses into long delays.

---

## 7. Use Group Size Deliberately

Group size affects almost every pace variable.

### Twosomes

**Best for:** premium events, qualifiers, strong pace control
**Pace effect:** fastest
**Tradeoff:** lowest field capacity

### Threesomes

**Best for:** many competitive tournaments
**Pace effect:** strong balance of speed and capacity
**Tradeoff:** slightly lower capacity than foursomes

### Foursomes

**Best for:** social and team events, maximum capacity
**Pace effect:** highest risk of delay
**Tradeoff:** strongest social benefit but most operational strain

### Policy Recommendation

Do not use foursomes by default. Use them only when the event’s purpose justifies the pace cost and the course can absorb it.

---

## 8. Match Pace Expectations to the Tournament Format

Different formats create different pace pressures.

### Scramble

Can move well, but often slows because teams discuss every shot, line up multiple putts, and may use contests or mulligans.

**Organizer implication:** pace can be good only if the event limits extras and keeps procedures simple.

### Stroke Play

Every shot counts, and players must finish holes fully unless a maximum score rule applies.

**Organizer implication:** pace expectations should be firm, and design choices should be disciplined.

### Best Ball / Four-Ball

Can be faster than full aggregate stroke play, but scorekeeping and hole completion still matter.

**Organizer implication:** avoid assuming team formats are automatically quick.

### Match Play

Can move well in smaller fields but may slow if players are uncertain about procedures or if many matches remain active late.

**Organizer implication:** clear rules and active oversight matter.

### Policy Recommendation

Pace standards should reflect the actual format, not just the fact that the event is a golf tournament.

---

## 9. Consider the Course Itself

The same field can play at very different speeds on different courses.

### Course Factors That Affect Pace

* long green-to-tee transitions
* forced carries or penalty-heavy opening holes
* difficult green complexes
* blind landing areas
* narrow target areas
* heavy rough
* long walks or drives between holes
* steep terrain
* known bottleneck holes

### Questions to Ask the Course

* Where does play usually back up?
* Are there specific holes that consistently slow rounds?
* What round time is realistic for this field type?
* Are there course setup choices that will materially affect pace?

### Policy Recommendation

Calibrate the pace policy to the actual host course, not to a generic idea of how long 18 holes “should” take.

---

## 10. Course Setup Can Protect or Damage Pace

Organizers often focus on pin positions and tee markers as competitive choices, but they are also pace decisions.

### Setup Choices That May Slow Play

* excessively long tees for the field ability
* harsh pin positions on severe slopes
* heavy rough in player landing areas
* frequent lost-ball risk
* difficult bunker placements for a weaker field

### Setup Choices That Support Better Pace

* tees matched to player ability
* fair but manageable hole locations
* clear markings and local rules
* practical setup for weather conditions

### Policy Recommendation

Set up the course to challenge the field appropriately, not to overwhelm it. Pace deteriorates quickly when the course asks players to hit shots they cannot realistically execute.

---

## 11. Cart Policy and Pace Are Connected

Cart use can either improve or worsen pace depending on how it is managed.

### Carts Can Improve Pace By

* reducing transit time between shots
* helping less mobile players keep up
* making long transfers easier
* allowing staff to intervene more effectively

### Carts Can Hurt Pace By

* creating rider inefficiency when players travel to opposite balls
* increasing congestion near tees and greens
* slowing play under cart-path-only restrictions
* producing confusion if assignments are poorly staged

### Policy Recommendation

Do not assume carts automatically solve pace issues. The cart plan must match the course layout, the field, and the start model.

---

## 12. Set a Realistic Target Round Time

A pace policy needs a measurable standard.

### Why a Target Time Matters

Without a target, “keep up the pace” is too vague to guide behavior or justify intervention.

### Good Pace Standards Should Be

* realistic for the event type
* consistent with the course and group size
* clear enough that staff can enforce them
* communicated before and during the event

### Policy Recommendation

Publish a target round time or checkpoint expectation appropriate to the event. That target should reflect what the course can actually support under tournament conditions, not an unrealistic best-case number.

---

## 13. Use Position Relative to the Group Ahead, Not Just Total Time

Total round time matters, but position on the course is often a more useful operational measure.

### Why This Matters

A group can be technically within a total-time standard and still create delay if it has lost position. Likewise, a group might be slightly over total time but still be keeping up properly because the entire field has slowed under the same conditions.

### Strong Pace Standard

Groups should be expected to:

* maintain reasonable time
* keep up with the group in front
* avoid creating an unnecessary gap behind the group ahead

### Policy Recommendation

Use both **target time** and **position relative to the group ahead** when evaluating pace.

---

## 14. Identify the Most Common Causes of Delay

A useful pace policy helps organizers recognize why groups fall behind.

### Common Player-Level Causes

* excessive pre-shot routines
* too much discussion before every shot
* long ball searches
* late club selection
* slow movement to the next shot
* scoring delays on or near greens
* failure to play ready when appropriate

### Common Event-Level Causes

* tee-time spacing too tight
* too many foursomes
* slow or confusing first-tee procedures
* on-course contests causing stoppages
* difficult course setup for the field
* poor cart staging
* inadequate marshal presence
* weather-related restrictions

### Policy Recommendation

When pace breaks down, diagnose the cause before deciding on a response. Not every slow group is the main problem; sometimes the design of the event created the delay.

---

## 15. Use Player Communication to Prevent Pace Problems Early

Players should know what the pace expectations are before they tee off.

### Pre-Event Communication Should Clarify

* target round time or pace expectation
* whether ready golf principles apply where appropriate
* scorekeeping expectations
* local rules that reduce confusion
* any maximum score or pickup rule if one exists
* how marshals or officials may intervene

### Starter Announcements Should Reinforce

* first-tee expectations
* when players should be ready to play
* how to record scores efficiently
* who to contact with rules questions rather than waiting indecisively

### Policy Recommendation

Pace communication should happen before the first delay occurs. It should not begin only after the field is already behind.

---

## 16. Use Ready Golf Principles Where Appropriate

Ready golf is one of the simplest tools for improving pace, but it must be communicated thoughtfully.

### Ready Golf Typically Means

* the player who is ready and can play safely does so
* players prepare while others are hitting
* players move promptly to their next shot
* scores are recorded efficiently, often at the next tee

### Important Caution

Ready golf is especially useful in stroke play and casual or mixed events, but it should still respect safety, courtesy, and any format-specific procedures.

### Policy Recommendation

If the event supports ready golf, say so clearly. Do not assume all players interpret it the same way.

---

## 17. Decide Whether a Maximum Score Rule Is Needed

In some events, a maximum score rule can protect pace. In others, it weakens the competition too much.

### Where a Maximum Score Rule Helps

* beginner-friendly events
* mixed-skill member tournaments
* junior development events
* social formats where pace matters more than full medal-play purity

### Where It May Not Fit

* championships
* formal stroke play competitions
* qualifiers
* events where every stroke must count traditionally

### Policy Recommendation

Use a maximum score rule only when it genuinely supports the purpose of the event. Do not add it automatically, and do not reject it automatically either.

---

## 18. Use Marshals, Rules Staff, or Pace Officials Intentionally

Pace rarely improves just because the field knows someone might be watching. Staff roles should be defined clearly.

### Helpful Oversight Roles

* starter at the first tee
* marshal monitoring spacing and bottlenecks
* rules official resolving issues quickly
* operations staff coordinating with the course

### Good Marshal Practice

* identify slow positions early
* address groups calmly and clearly
* focus on position and flow, not just blame
* report recurring structural issues back to the organizer

### Policy Recommendation

Do not assign marshals only as symbolic presence. Give them a clear standard for when and how to intervene.

---

## 19. Decide How Intervention Will Work

A pace policy should specify what happens when groups fall behind.

### Common Intervention Levels

#### Level 1: Informal Reminder

A group is drifting but can likely recover.

**Action:** courteous reminder to close the gap or improve efficiency

#### Level 2: Formal Pace Warning

A group has clearly lost position or exceeded the standard.

**Action:** clear notification that the group is behind and expected to recover promptly

#### Level 3: Active Pace Management

The group is materially affecting the field.

**Action:** increased monitoring, instructions to accelerate procedures, possible operational adjustments depending on event rules

### Policy Recommendation

Decide in advance how pace intervention works. A policy should not force marshals to improvise under pressure.

---

## 20. Distinguish Between Casual Guidance and Competitive Enforcement

The right response depends on the event.

### Social or Charity Event

The pace approach is usually more educational and operational than punitive.

**Best focus:** encouragement, structural fixes, course flow, and simplified procedures

### Competitive Event

The pace approach may need clearer enforcement and more formal monitoring.

**Best focus:** published standards, consistent intervention, fairness to the rest of the field

### Policy Recommendation

Use the lightest intervention that still protects the event, but do not allow repeated delay to harm every other group.

---

## 21. Plan for Pace Pressure Points on the Course

Some parts of the course deserve special attention.

### Common Pace Pressure Points

* early par 3s
* reachable par 5s with layup indecision
* difficult par 4s with forced carries
* turn areas with food or rest stops
* holes near contest activations
* finishing stretches where leader boards or scoring tension increase delay

### Policy Recommendation

If the course has known bottlenecks, assign staff attention there rather than waiting for backups to spread through the entire field.

---

## 22. Weather, Turf Conditions, and Pace Are Connected

Bad weather does not just affect safety. It can also change pace significantly.

### Pace Impacts of Weather Conditions

* cart-path-only rules slow transit
* wet turf increases plugging and lost-ball searches
* wind increases shot difficulty and decision time
* heat slows field movement and increases fatigue

### Policy Recommendation

If conditions will likely slow the field materially, adjust expectations early rather than pretending the original pace standard is still realistic.

---

## 23. Use Post-Round Review to Improve Future Events

Pace policy should not end when the round ends.

### Useful Debrief Questions

* Where did the field first begin to back up?
* Was the issue caused by format, setup, interval, staffing, or player behavior?
* Did the target time match reality?
* Were marshals placed effectively?
* Did scorekeeping or contests slow the field?
* Would smaller groups or different intervals have improved flow?

### Policy Recommendation

Treat pace performance as an event metric to review after every tournament.

---

## 24. Recommended Default Policies by Event Type

### Charity or Corporate Tournament

* use conservative pace expectations
* simplify contests and add-ons
* communicate ready golf clearly
* use marshals to preserve flow, not just enforce rules
* prioritize keeping the round enjoyable without allowing major drift

### Member Tournament

* publish clear target times
* use group sizes and intervals that match field ability
* intervene early when groups lose position
* review whether maximum score or modified procedures would help future editions

### Competitive Stroke Play Event

* use disciplined intervals and smaller groups where possible
* publish both target time and position expectations
* employ active marshal or official oversight
* treat pace as part of competitive fairness, not merely convenience

### Premium Invitational

* prioritize smooth flow and comfort
* avoid overloading the course
* accept slightly lower capacity if it materially improves the pace experience

---

## 25. Common Mistakes to Avoid

* blaming players for a tee sheet that was too aggressive
* using foursomes by default in events that need faster flow
* failing to publish a measurable pace standard
* focusing only on total time and ignoring lost position
* overloading the event with contests or extras
* using marshals without clear authority or guidance
* setting the course up beyond the field’s practical ability
* failing to adjust pace expectations when weather or restrictions change
* treating slow play as a small annoyance rather than a tournament-wide issue

---

## 26. Recommended Default Guideline

If an organizer wants a strong general starting point, the following is a practical default:

* Build the event so that the field has a realistic chance to maintain pace through **appropriate group size, tee spacing, and format choices**.
* Publish a **target round time** and evaluate groups by both elapsed time and **position relative to the group ahead**.
* Communicate ready golf, scorekeeping expectations, and key pace standards before the round begins.
* Use marshals or officials to identify lost position early and intervene in a calm, consistent way.
* Review course setup, cart policy, contests, and weather restrictions as pace variables rather than treating pace as only a player problem.
* Adjust future tournaments based on where and why pace broke down.

---

## 27. Final Advice

A good pace-of-play policy is not about pressuring players to rush. It is about helping the organizer create a tournament that flows properly from the first tee to the final scorecard.

The best pace outcomes come from clear design, realistic expectations, and calm intervention when needed. When pace is managed well, players usually do not describe the event as “fast.” They describe it as smooth, organized, and enjoyable. That is the standard worth aiming for.

A tournament organizer should therefore see pace of play as one of the clearest signals of overall event quality. If pace works, many other things work more easily as well.
